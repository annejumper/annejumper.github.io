During my Summer 2025 appointment at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory under the Department of Energy’s Science Undergraduate Laboratory Internship (SULI) program, I worked under the mentorship of Gao Liu, Alvaro Videla, and Xiuyu Jin. Their research focuses on understanding dendrite growth in solid-state batteries (SSBs) through modeling and operando optical microscopy. Dendrites are needle-like lithium structures that grow from the anode to the cathode resulting in battery failure. Preventing their formation is essential to the commercialization of SSBs, which is vital to electrifying transportation and, thus, achieving climate and economic goals.

My work entailed using COMSOL Multiphysics® to model the current density distributions in solid-state cells with various electrode shapes, separation distances, and electrolyte materials. Since dendrites grow preferentially in high current density regions, understanding how electrode geometry changes these distributions can help in designing more reliable batteries. I also gained experience in electrode and electrolyte fabrication, coin cell assembly, cell cycling, data analysis, and using lab equipment like the glovebox, mill, and crimper. It was rewarding to contribute to both the computational and experimental sides of battery research.

Through my project, we identified electrode geometries that concentrate current in predictable ways and recognized geometric features that may suppress dendrite growth. In arriving at these outcomes, I learned that failure is both a valuable and expected part of science. Initial difficulties with non-functioning batteries and simulation instability became rewarding mechanisms for learning. Most importantly, I saw the value of interdisciplinary collaboration in research. The lab environment exposed me to individuals with backgrounds in metallurgy, engineering, electrochemistry, and economics. Engaging with experts in graphite recycling and lithium manufacturing and presenting my findings in formal meetings aided my professional growth. My experience has deepened my appreciation for collaborative science and reinforced my resolve to work in developing clean energy technologies.
